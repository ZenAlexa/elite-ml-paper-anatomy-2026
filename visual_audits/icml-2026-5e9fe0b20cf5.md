## Figure audits

### Figure 1 — p. 2, introduction, main, single-column

Three compact side-by-side DAG panels `(a) Confounder`, `(b) Collider`, `(c) Mediator` use circular X/Y/B nodes, directed arrows, and a gray node for latent conditioning. It is a vector conceptual diagram with no axes or legend; labels are direct and the repeated grammar makes the three bias structures comparable. The palette is primarily black/gray and grayscale-safe. Caption: “Figure 1. Canonical causal structures that induce dataset bias. Grey nodes indicate latent conditioning.” The caption gives title, setup, and the gray-node encoding, but no empirical finding; it is not fully self-contained without the surrounding causal discussion. This introduces the causal-bias problem and feeds the SAM interface in Figure 2.

### Figure 2 — p. 3, theory, main, single-column

A centered SAM DAG places Y, X, W, Z, and Ŷ in a compact directed graph. It is a vector conceptual diagram with no axes, legend, or uncertainty display; black/gray nodes and arrows are direct-labelled and grayscale-safe. Caption: “Figure 2. SAM graph. Y is the target, Z are variables on active, non-directed paths between X and Y, and W are mediator variables assumed to be unwanted shortcuts. Ŷ denotes the prediction from a fixed prediction model. See appendix Fig. D.1 for more details on permitted relationships.” The caption defines the symbols, states the unwanted-shortcut interpretation, and points to Figure D.1, but does not state a result. It is the method/theory bridge for the conditional-independence criterion.

### Figure 3 — p. 7, experimental design, main, single-column

Five small dataset DAGs are arranged as a 3-over-2 composite for dSprites, Blob Dataset, YaleB, FairFace, and Waterbirds. Each panel names variables and paths; blue marks the task-relevant path from target to image, with additional dark/colored arrows for other causal or bias relations. It is a vector conceptual-diagram grid without axes or legend. The direct labels and panel titles support comparison, but blue/red path semantics are not fully redundant and the figure is not reliably grayscale-safe. Caption: “Figure 3. Causal graphs used in our experiments across different datasets. Blue path means causally task-relevant, going from target to our input (image).” It supplies setup and one color key, but no numerical finding. It defines the experimental causal structures used by Table 1 and Table 2.

### Figure 4 — p. 8, results, main, single-column

### Figure D.1 — p. 16, Appendix D, page-width

A large vector anti-causal DAG uses nested/boxed Z and W groups, Y, X, Ŷ, S, ellipses, and many directed/non-directed connections. It has no axes or legend; direct labels and mostly gray/black strokes make it grayscale-safe, although the dense graph is difficult to parse at page scale. Caption: “Figure D.1. The main setting we consider in anti-causal prediction. Y is the target we want to predict using X only. W is a set of variables that only permits mediated effects from Y to X. Z contains nodes that lie on open paths between Y and X but are non-directed, i.e. any kind of nodes that lie on open back-door paths between X and Y, or any kinds of paths that are opened due to conditioned colliders. Nodes in Z are allowed to have arbitrary connections in between them, as long as Z stays a valid adjustment set for (Y,X), and the graph is still a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Nodes in W are mediators. Nodes in S are variables that are d-separation irrelevant between Y and X (and Y and Ŷ respectively). We can always ignore the variables in S for our settings as they do not harm or inform anything for our prediction setup. Thus, these variables are omitted in our analysis and discussions in the main manuscript.” It is self-contained as a graph specification and anchors the permitted relationships referenced by Figure 2 and the proofs.

## Cross-object judgment

The visual narrative is causal: Figures 1–2 define the bias/SAM mechanism, Figure 3 and Table 1 establish dataset and capability coverage, Figure 4 and Table 3 operationalize controlled counterfactual testing, and Table 2 presents the headline six-dataset comparison. Appendix Figures D.1/E.2 provide the formal graph/proof substrate; H.3–H.8 make dataset and counterfactual constructions concrete; Tables 4–7 expose the corrected implementation, search cost, counterfactual decomposition, and runtime/OOM boundary.

The most reusable pattern is the pairing of a compact causal diagram with a table whose metric directions and data-generating conditions are explicit, followed by an appendix qualitative intervention grid that makes the mechanism inspectable. The highest-value objects are Table 2 (broad comparison), Figure 4 + Table 3 (mechanism test), Figure H.8 + Table 6 (counterfactual interpretation), and Table 7 (efficiency/failure). Main weaknesses are the absence of exact public plotting/table source, small dense DAG labels, incomplete grayscale redundancy for colored causal paths and photographic examples, and missing run/seed or sampling details in several captions. Overall, the paper uses restrained native TeX diagrams/tables to connect causal assumptions to performance, then uses appendix counterfactual images and runtime tables to explain why the headline comparison should be trusted.
